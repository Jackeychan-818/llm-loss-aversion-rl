\section{Overview}

This document describes the end-to-end training pipeline used to fine-tune
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) so that it
makes more rational trade decisions---i.e., decisions based on which good is
genuinely better rather than on which good it happens to hold.

The pipeline has four stages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Construct a dataset of trade scenarios with ground-truth utility differences $\tilde{\delta}$
    \item Define a reward function that scores each model response against $\tilde{\delta}$
    \item Run GRPO to update the model's LoRA parameters using those rewards
    \item Evaluate the fine-tuned model on a held-out test set and estimate $\hat{\lambda}_{\text{after}}$
\end{enumerate}

% =============================================================================
\section{Experimental Paradigm}

Each training example presents the model with an \emph{endowment-effect} trade
scenario. The model is endowed with good $X$ (or $Y$) and asked whether it
would trade for the other good.

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=gray!10, title=Example Prompt]
\textit{Suppose that, by chance, you receive a \textbf{shoe polish} out of many
possible goods you could have received. In terms of finish, it is shiny; in
terms of formula, it is enriched. You can keep the shoe polish or trade it for
a \textbf{highlighter}. In terms of saturation, the highlighter is bold; in
terms of tip, it is firm durable. Would you trade it? Answer with the single
word Yes or No.}
\end{tcolorbox}

Each case is run from \emph{both} perspectives:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{X-perspective:} endowed with $X$, offered $Y$
    \item \textbf{Y-perspective:} endowed with $Y$, offered $X$
\end{itemize}

A rational agent's decision should depend only on which good is genuinely
better, not on which one it currently holds. The loss aversion coefficient
$\lambda$ measures how much the model's choices deviate from this rational
benchmark---a higher $\lambda$ means the model disproportionately refuses to
give up whatever it holds.

Training and compositional evaluation use the same 100 goods and the same
4,945 non-trial goods pairs. For each pair, \texttt{remaining\_goods.json}

exact questions/configurations, not goods or pair identities.

Each scenario involves two goods described by up to four attributes drawn from
\texttt{everyday\_goods\_full.json} (the master item catalogue). Attributes
take ordinal values (1, 2, or 3) representing low, medium, and high quality on
each dimension.

\section{Ground-Truth Utility Differences ($\tilde{\delta}$)}

\subsection{Why We Need $\tilde{\delta}$}

To reward rational behavior, we need to know \emph{which good is actually
better} in each scenario. We operationalize this as the utility difference
$\delta = U_X - U_Y$, where $U_X$ and $U_Y$ are the latent utilities of goods
$X$ and $Y$ respectively. A positive $\delta$ means $X$ is genuinely better;
negative means $Y$ is better.

\subsection{Estimating $\tilde{\delta}$ from Qwen's Own Responses (Primary)}

Utility is agent-specific, so our primary signal is Qwen's \emph{own} estimated
preference ordering: we ask whether post-training can remove ownership
dependence while preserving the model's own ordering, rather than imposing an
ordering aggregated from other models. Both signals are pseudo-utilities, not
objective cardinal ground truth over heterogeneous goods.

A natural objection is that base Qwen answers ``No'' to roughly 99\% of endowed
prompts, which might leave its utilities weakly identified. Identification
survives because we score with \emph{log-probabilities} at link scale
$T=1$ rather than binary choices: $P(\text{Yes})=0.08$ and $P(\text{Yes})=0.28$
both decode to ``No'' but carry substantially different information about
relative utility. Under a binary ($T=0$) encoding the low trade rate genuinely
would flatten $\tilde{\delta}$ into noise; under the continuous encoding it does
not. Empirically, the item and attribute coefficients are well identified
(91.9\% of $\alpha$ and 100\% of $\beta$ significant; median $|t|$ of 9.6 and
27.5), and $\tilde{\delta}$ is well spread (sd $0.94$; only 1.6\% near zero).

As an independent check, we compare $\operatorname{sign}(\tilde{\delta})$ with
Qwen's \emph{frozen ownership-free} preferences, elicited over multiple
paraphrases and both display orders. The two signals are derived independently,
and they agree on 71.1\% of held-out cases (70.8\% on training cases) against a
50\% chance baseline. Agreement increases with magnitude---85.5\% when
$|\tilde{\delta}|>1.0$ versus 63.6\% when $|\tilde{\delta}|\le 0.5$---and since
the reward is weighted by $|\tilde{\delta}|$, it is loudest precisely where the
signal is most trustworthy.

\subsection{Reward-Source Ablation: $\tilde{\delta}$ from Frontier Models}

As a reward-source ablation, we also estimate $\delta$ from six frontier models
that trade at reasonable rates: GPT-4o, GPT-5, GPT-5.2, Gemini 1.5 Pro,
Llama-70B, and DeepSeek-R1. (Claude, Apertus-70B, and GPT-3.5 are excluded due
to $<$5\% Yes-rates, which leaves their utility estimates poorly identified.)
This variant imposes an externally aggregated preference ordering and serves to
isolate the contribution of the reward's source. We do not claim the Qwen-own
signal dominates it on every axis: it attains lower $\hat{\lambda}$, whereas the
consensus signal attains lower $\hat{\eta}$ and higher OOD consistency.

For each frontier model $m$, we run the structural NLS estimation
(Model A) and obtain item- and attribute-level coefficients
$\hat{\alpha}^{(m)}, \hat{\beta}^{(m)}$. The utility of a good $g$ under model
$m$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{U}^{(m)}_g = \hat{\alpha}^{(m)}_{g} +
    \sum_{k=1}^{2} \hat{\beta}^{(m)}_{k, a_{gk}}
\end{equation}
where $a_{gk}$ is the attribute level of good $g$ on dimension $k$.

The \emph{consensus} utility difference for case $i$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\delta}_i = \frac{1}{6} \sum_{m=1}^{6}
    \left(\hat{U}^{(m)}_{X_i} - \hat{U}^{(m)}_{Y_i}\right)
\end{equation}

All 59,400 values of $\tilde{\delta}$ are precomputed and stored in
\texttt{data/deltas/delta\_consensus\_v3.json}. Roughly 64\% of cases have
unanimous sign agreement across all six models; the remaining 36\% use the
mean $\tilde{\delta}$, which naturally receives lower magnitude and thus lower
gradient weight.

\section{Reward Function}

\subsection{Rational Answer}

Given $\tilde{\delta}_i = \hat{U}_{X_i} - \hat{U}_{Y_i}$, the rational
answer for each perspective is:

\begin{equation}
    r^*_i =
    \begin{cases}
        \texttt{No}  & \text{if perspective} = X \text{ and } \tilde{\delta}_i > 0
                       \quad \text{(keep } X\text{, it is better)} \\
        \texttt{Yes} & \text{if perspective} = X \text{ and } \tilde{\delta}_i < 0
                       \quad \text{(trade for } Y\text{, it is better)} \\
        \texttt{Yes} & \text{if perspective} = Y \text{ and } \tilde{\delta}_i > 0
                       \quad \text{(trade for } X\text{, it is better)} \\
        \texttt{No}  & \text{if perspective} = Y \text{ and } \tilde{\delta}_i < 0
                       \quad \text{(keep } Y\text{, it is better)}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Reward Formula}

The reward for a model response $\hat{r}_i$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \mathcal{R}(\hat{r}_i,\, \tilde{\delta}_i) =
    \begin{cases}
        +|\tilde{\delta}_i| & \text{if } \hat{r}_i = r^*_i \\
        -|\tilde{\delta}_i| & \text{if } \hat{r}_i \neq r^*_i
    \end{cases}
    }
\end{equation}

Using $|\tilde{\delta}|$ as the magnitude (rather than a flat $\pm 1$) has two
desirable properties:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Confidence weighting.} Cases where all six frontier models
    agree strongly produce large $|\tilde{\delta}|$ and thus stronger gradient
    updates. Ambiguous cases (where $\tilde{\delta} \approx 0$) contribute
    little regardless of the model's answer---appropriate, since the ``correct''
    answer is itself uncertain.
    \item \textbf{Naturalness.} The reward scale is tied to the utility
    difference, not an arbitrary constant, which keeps the reward signal
    stationary across different types of goods.
\end{enumerate}

Responses that cannot be parsed as ``Yes'' or ``No'' are treated as irrational
and receive $-|\tilde{\delta}|$.

\section{GRPO Training Algorithm}

\subsection{Background}

GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization, \citealt{shao2024deepseekmath}) is a
policy-gradient algorithm that eliminates the learned value network required by
PPO. This is appropriate here because our reward is a clean verifiable signal
that does not require a separate critic to estimate.

\subsection{Algorithm}

At each training step, given a prompt $q_i$:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{GRPO Update Step}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State Sample $G = 16$ completions $\{o_1, \ldots, o_G\}$ from the current policy $\pi_\theta$ at temperature 1.5
\State Score each completion: $\mathcal{R}_j = \mathcal{R}(o_j, \tilde{\delta}_i)$ for $j = 1, \ldots, G$
\State \textbf{Zero-diversity groups:} if all $G$ completions are identical, the task rewards are set to $0$ \Comment{the group is not skipped; see \S\ref{sec:zero-adv}}
\State Compute group-centered advantages:
    \[
        A_j = \mathcal{R}_j - \frac{1}{G}\sum_{k=1}^{G}\mathcal{R}_k
        \quad \text{(mean-centered, no std normalization; \texttt{scale\_rewards: none})}
    \]
\State Compute clipped policy-gradient loss (DAPO variant):
    \[
        \mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}} = -\frac{1}{G} \sum_{j=1}^{G}
        \min\!\left(
            \frac{\pi_\theta(o_j \mid q_i)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_j \mid q_i)} A_j,\;
            \text{clip}\!\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(o_j \mid q_i)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_j \mid q_i)},\,
            1-\varepsilon,\, 1+\varepsilon\right) A_j
        \right)
    \]
\State Add KL penalty: $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}} + \beta \cdot D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}})$
\State Update $\theta$ via gradient descent
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Zero Task-Reward Advantage Groups}
\label{sec:zero-adv}

Because the base model says ``No'' 99\% of the time, most early groups of
$G=16$ completions are entirely ``No''---every response is identical. When all
rewards within a group are equal, mean-centering gives $A_j = 0$ for all $j$,
so the group contributes no policy-gradient signal. We refer to these as
\emph{zero task-reward advantage groups}. Empirically the logged
\texttt{frac\_reward\_zero\_std} sits near $0.8$, so most optimizer steps are of
this kind.

Our reward function returns all-zero task rewards for such groups. Three points
are easy to state imprecisely, so we state them explicitly:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Nothing is skipped.} We use the stock TRL \texttt{GRPOTrainer}
    (v1.3.0) without subclassing, so a zero-diversity group still consumes
    generation compute, still runs the backward pass, and still triggers an
    optimizer step. This is not generation-level dynamic sampling in the sense of
    DAPO \citep{yu2025dapo}, which resamples until a group has reward variance;
    implementing that would require overriding completion generation.
    \item \textbf{Zeroing the rewards is not what makes the advantage zero.} An
    all-identical group already has a constant reward vector, so mean-centering
    would give $A_j = 0$ regardless. The all-zero return therefore changes the
    \emph{logged} mean reward---such groups are recorded as $0$ rather than their
    true $\pm|\tilde{\delta}_i|$---but not the gradient. This is one reason the
    training-reward curve is treated as a diagnostic only, never as a
    checkpoint-selection criterion; checkpoints are selected on held-out
    structural $\hat{\lambda}$.
    \item \textbf{Zero task advantage is not a zero update.} With
    $\beta = 0.04$ the objective retains its KL term,
    $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}} + \beta\,D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}})$.
    When $A_j = 0$ the policy-gradient term vanishes but the KL term does not, so
    these steps still apply a gradient pulling the policy toward the reference.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Training Infrastructure}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hardware:} 1$\times$ NVIDIA A100-40GB SXM on NSCC ASPIRE 2A
    \item \textbf{Base model:} Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (LoRA fine-tuning, rank 16)
    \item \textbf{Library:} TRL 1.3.0 \texttt{GRPOTrainer} + PEFT
    \item \textbf{Training steps:} 98,900 (one full epoch over \texttt{remaining\_goods.json})
    \item \textbf{Wall time:} $\approx$7 days across three sequential 72h PBS jobs, resuming from checkpoints every 200 steps
    \item \textbf{Generation speed:} $\approx$6--8 seconds/step (plain HF \texttt{generate}, no vLLM)
\end{itemize}

\section{Evaluation}

\subsection{Scoring Method}

After training, the fine-tuned model is evaluated on
\texttt{test\_goods.json}: 9,890 exact attribute configurations that were not
used for training, constructed from familiar goods and goods pairs. For each
case and each perspective (X and Y), hard responses are generated greedily
(\texttt{do\_sample=False}), corresponding to decoding temperature
$T_{\mathrm{dec}}=0$. The structural estimates do not use sampled response
frequencies. Instead, we deterministically score the candidate continuations
``Yes'' and ``No'' under the model's native, unscaled logits:
\begin{equation}
    P_\theta(\text{Yes}\mid x,\{\text{Yes},\text{No}\})
    =
    \frac{\exp(s_{\text{Yes}})}
    {\exp(s_{\text{Yes}})+\exp(s_{\text{No}})},
    \qquad
    s_a=\sum_t \log p_\theta(a_t\mid x,a_{<t}).
\end{equation}
This gives exact normalized probabilities for ``Yes'' and ``No'' without
sampling noise or temperature rescaling, matching the log-probability format
used for the pre-training baseline ($\hat{\lambda}_{\text{before}}$).

\subsection{Structural Estimation}

Choice probabilities are fed into the Model A (NLS) structural estimator from
\texttt{eval/core\_exp\_refactored.py}. The model estimates:
\begin{equation}
    z_i = (1 + \lambda)\,e^{U_{X_i}} - e^{U_{Y_i}} + \eta + \varepsilon_i
\end{equation}
jointly over all cases, with item and attribute fixed effects absorbing
heterogeneity across goods. In the implementation, the estimator is invoked
with \texttt{T=1} to retain the continuous probabilities and use the normalized
logistic link. This internal estimator flag is not the model's decoding
temperature; evaluation decoding remains greedy ($T_{\mathrm{dec}}=0$).

\subsection{Attribute sensitivity on the held-out configurations}

We test whether the disjoint attribute configurations produce substantively
different decisions. For each goods pair and endowment perspective, we first
difference the two held-out configurations. This removes pair-by-perspective
fixed factors and leaves eight endowed-minus-offered indicators for the
non-reference $3\times3$ ordinal attribute profiles. The profiles jointly
predict the change in $P(\mathrm{No})$ under pair-clustered inference
($\chi^2(8)=5{,}097.22$, $p<10^{-1000}$). A more conservative
leave-one-good-out jackknife gives $\chi^2(8)=756.05$ and $p<10^{-150}$;
the smallest absolute jackknife $z$-statistic is 11.23.

The effect is practically large. Attribute profiles explain 48.8\% of the
within-pair, within-perspective variation. Changing the reserved configuration
flips the X-perspective response for 34.07\% of pairs and the Y-perspective
response for 34.24\%; at least one response flips for 42.18\% of pairs. In a
complementary levels regression with item-difference fixed effects, adding the
attribute profiles reduces five-fold pair-grouped prediction error by 47.43\%.
These diagnostics rule out a constant Yes/No policy and support
within-benchmark generalization to unseen configurations. They do not test
zero-shot transfer to unseen goods or categories. The coefficients represent
generic ordinal profiles rather than separately identified semantic attributes
such as flavor, freshness, or scent. A separate 50-good OOD suite may be
reported as external-validity evidence; no additional OOD run is required for
the core configuration-generalization claim.

\subsection{Claims We Do Not Make}

The primary model attains $\hat{\lambda} = 0.111$ (SE $0.014$), close to the
ownership-neutral target $\lambda = 0$. We deliberately stop short of three
comparisons that the current evidence cannot support.

\textbf{We do not claim superiority over human loss aversion.} The commonly
cited $\lambda \approx 2.25$ \citep{tversky1992advances} is estimated from a
different prospect-theory task under a different parameterization; here the
endowed-good multiplier is $1 + \lambda$. The two quantities are not on a
common scale, so ``below the human benchmark'' would be a category error rather
than a finding.

\textbf{We do not claim superiority over frontier models.} The Phase-0 frontier
estimates have not been harmonized with ours across model endpoints, sample
sizes, or probability scorers. Until a harmonized comparison is run, any
frontier ranking would confound the intervention with measurement differences.

\textbf{We do claim a before/after reduction, but only against the matched
base.} Every row of the Results table shares one pipeline---the exact local
weights, the same 9{,}890 rows, the same teacher-forced scorer, and the same
estimator---so the reduction from $\hat{\lambda} = 7.637$ to $0.111$ is
attributable to training. We do \emph{not} report the earlier $\lambda = 11.75$
as the baseline: it measured a different endpoint, sample, and scorer, and
roughly 54\% of it reflects that mismatch rather than the model. Quoting it
would inflate the reduction with a measurement artifact.

Accordingly the claim we make is narrower and matched to the evidence: relative
to the ownership-neutral target $\lambda = 0$, post-training with a frozen,
model-specific pseudo-utility substantially reduces measured endowment
dependence on held-out attribute configurations, and retains low loss aversion
on goods excluded from fine-tuning---reported jointly with $\eta$, choice
consistency, and preference preservation, since $\lambda$ alone can be
misleading when $\eta$ absorbs choice rigidity.
