Loss-Aversion RL · Out-of-Distribution Evaluation

Do λ and η hold up on goods the model never saw?

Three models scored on a frozen suite of 50 brand-new goods (10 new categories, zero overlap with the training set). We report the structural loss-aversion estimates and the raw Yes/No choice patterns.

Suite: 9,800 structural cases Estimator: NLS (Model A), T=1 Treatment: baseline Endowment: both perspectives per pair

The short version

  • The debiasing generalizes. Both fine-tuned models trade actively and make consistent, preference-driven choices on goods they were never trained on — while the base model refuses to trade in 99.96% of cases.
  • λ stays low out-of-distribution (0.23–0.40) vs. a base that is entirely endowment-locked. A mild generalization gap: λ roughly doubles from in-distribution, but the core effect holds.
  • The base λ̂ = 2.21 is not a reliable number — with no trades and no perspective asymmetry, λ is weakly identified and the rigidity is absorbed into η̂ = 5.36. Read the base by its behavior, not its λ. (See caveat below.)

⚠ Why the base λ̂ is not comparable

λ is identified by the asymmetry between the two endowment perspectives — whether the model refuses a trade specifically because it holds the good. The base model refuses uniformly (P(Yes) ≈ 0.5% from both sides; asymmetry ≈ 0.0003), so there is almost no signal to pin λ down.

With nothing to separate them, the estimator loads the near-total refusal onto the blanket status-quo term (η̂ = 5.36) and leaves λ̂ = 2.21 loosely constrained — its SE (0.261) is ~180× the fine-tuned models'. The base is maximally rigid; that rigidity simply lives in η here, not λ.

Reporting guidance: don't place the base λ̂ on the leaderboard as if comparable. State the base by its behavior — 0 / 9,800 trades, η̂ ≈ 5.4 — and reserve the λ comparison for the fine-tuned models, whose estimates are genuinely identified.
